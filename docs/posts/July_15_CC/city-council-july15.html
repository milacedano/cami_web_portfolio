What Chicagoans Should Know from the July 15 City Council Meeting

Referendums, EV charging, housing, public space, and other decisions that could shape Chicago

public policy
featured
Author

Maria Camila Cedano

Published

June 19, 2026

Chicagoans could soon be voting on whether the city should bring back gunshot-detection technology. At the same time, City Council is moving forward with curbside EV charging, affordable housing developments, new neighborhood public spaces, and changes to how residents can access government records.

Those were just some of the decisions to come out of the July 15, 2026 Chicago City Council meeting.

With hundreds of items moving through City Council, it can be difficult to tell which decisions actually matter beyond City Hall. So instead of going through every parking permit, zoning change, and appointment, here are some of the decisions from the meeting that Chicagoans should know about.

Chicagoans will vote on some big questions this November

Two citywide advisory questions are headed to Chicago voters for the November 3, 2026 general election.

Should Chicago bring back gunshot-detection technology?

City Council adopted R2026-0025451, placing a question on the ballot asking whether Chicago should restart the use of gunshot-detection technology that alerts first responders when gunfire is detected.

The question puts the debate over gunshot-detection technology directly before Chicago voters.

Should Chicago create a permanent festival and event space?

Council also adopted R2026-0025459, asking voters whether Chicago should develop a permanent outdoor space for festivals and other large-scale events.

While the referendum itself does not build a venue, it gives Chicagoans an opportunity to weigh in on whether the city should pursue the idea.

The 26th Ward will also get its own ballot question

Residents of the 26th Ward will have an additional local question on their ballots.

SR2025-0021045 asks voters whether shared housing units in the ward should be limited.

Unlike the first two questions, this one is specific to voters in the 26th Ward.

Chicago is making changes to how we drive, park, and charge EVs

Transportation was another important part of the meeting, with Council approving changes involving electric vehicles, speed limits, parking meters, and street regulations.

Curbside EV charging is coming

Council passed O2026-0025394, establishing an Electric Vehicle Curbside Charging Pilot Program.

The program creates a framework for electric vehicle charging infrastructure along Chicago streets.

This matters because many Chicago residents, particularly those living in apartments or homes without garages, do not have access to private charging infrastructure. Curbside charging could eventually make owning an electric vehicle more practical for residents who rely on street parking.

New gas stations will face additional review

Council also passed O2026-0025429.

The ordinance requires special-use approval for new gas stations. It also applies to additions to existing gas stations involving seven or more electric vehicle chargers.

That means certain projects will have to go through an additional approval process rather than automatically being allowed under existing zoning rules.

Some streets are getting 20 mph speed limits

Several traffic changes were approved, including new 20 mph speed limits.

One ordinance establishes a 20 mph limit on portions of streets around 65th through 70th Streets, including sections of Harvard, Stewart, Normal, Parnell, and Eggleston.

Another establishes a 20 mph limit on North California Avenue between Roscoe Street and Addison Street, while another does the same on North Kedzie Avenue between Schubert Avenue and Diversey Avenue.

Other traffic changes approved during the meeting included new stop signs, parking restrictions, residential permit parking, and changes to parking meters.

City-owned land is being used for new housing and development

A significant group of ordinances involved the sale or transfer of City-owned land.

Several properties were sold through ChiBlockBuilder, the city’s program for returning vacant City-owned land to productive use.

Properties approved for sale included sites on:

  • South Giles Avenue
  • East 50th Street
  • South St. Lawrence Avenue
  • South Calumet Avenue
  • West Kamerling Avenue
  • South Martin Luther King Jr. Drive
  • South Langley Avenue
  • West 34th Place
  • West Adams Street and West Jackson Boulevard
  • North Pine Avenue

Some of these transactions are connected to larger housing initiatives.

Missing-middle housing

Council approved the sale of properties at 2438, 2442, 2444, and 2445 W. 34th Place to M3 Chicago LLC through the ChiBlockBuilder Missing Middle Infill Housing Initiative.

Another group of properties — including sites on West Adams Street, West Jackson Boulevard, South Homan Avenue, and West Gladys Avenue — was sold to Citizens Building a Better Community LLC through the same initiative.

Missing-middle housing generally refers to housing types that fall between detached single-family homes and large apartment buildings, such as duplexes, triplexes, townhomes, and smaller multifamily buildings.

Affordable housing in Morgan Park

Council also approved O2026-0026567, involving the sale of City-owned property at 11414 S. Halsted Street to Far South CDC.

The property is intended for the development of Morgan Park Commons (Phase 0) under the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit program.

Affordable housing in Bronzeville

Another ordinance approved an agreement involving HUD and parcels in Bronzeville associated with the Hearts United Phase II project.

The agreement allows parcels affected by HUD foreclosure to be reconveyed for affordable housing development.

Together, these measures show how City-owned land can become part of Chicago’s broader housing and neighborhood development strategy.

Six neighborhood public spaces are getting funding

Council approved the use of Open Space Impact Fee funds for six projects across Chicago.

The projects include:

Project Location
Chicago Farmworks 409 N. Albany Ave. and 411–419 N. Kedzie Ave.
Montrose Metra Garden 4350 N. Ravenswood Ave.
Goose Island Overlook 1111 N. Elston Ave.
Garfield Park Community Orchards 3001 W. Fifth Ave.
CircEsteem Plaza 4730 N. Sheridan Rd.
Kimball and Wellington Plaza N. Kimball Ave. and W. Wellington Ave.

These projects range from gardens and orchards to plazas and other public spaces.

While individual projects may appear small compared with major citywide legislation, investments like these can directly affect how residents experience their neighborhoods.

Development and preservation are reshaping parts of Chicago

Several other decisions dealt with economic development, historic preservation, and the physical development of neighborhoods.

The K Entrepreneurship Hub

Council approved O2026-0026260, a redevelopment agreement providing Neighborhood Opportunity Fund assistance for The K Entrepreneurship Hub Project at 4400 W. Madison Street.

The Neighborhood Opportunity Fund uses fees generated by certain downtown developments to support commercial and cultural projects in underserved neighborhoods.

The Chicago Daily News Building is now a landmark

Council approved the historic landmark designation of the Chicago Daily News Building at 2 N. Riverside Plaza / 400 W. Monroe Street.

The building was also the subject of a Class L property-tax incentive approved by Council.

The Class L program is designed to encourage the rehabilitation and preservation of qualifying historic buildings.

City government is changing some of its own rules

Not everything approved at the meeting involved physical development.

Some measures affect how Chicago government itself operates.

City Council videos must be downloadable

Council adopted R2026-0023364, changing City Council rules to require video recordings of City Council and committee meetings to be downloadable.

That may sound like a small technical change, but it makes public meeting records easier for residents, journalists, researchers, and community organizations to save, review, and analyze.

City officials face new restrictions involving prediction markets

Council passed SO2026-0025003, amending Chicago’s ethics rules.

The ordinance prohibits current or former City officials and employees from using or disclosing confidential City information for prediction markets or event-based contracts.

New transit board members were approved

Council approved several appointments to the boards overseeing public transportation in Chicago and northeastern Illinois.

The appointments included members of the Chicago Transit Board, Metra Commuter Rail Board, Pace Board, and Northern Illinois Transit Authority Board.

These appointments matter because these boards help make decisions about the agencies responsible for moving millions of people throughout the Chicago region.

Chicago has a new Public Health Commissioner

Council also approved the appointment of Dr. Garth Walker as Commissioner of the Chicago Department of Public Health.

Also worth knowing

A few other decisions from the meeting are worth highlighting.

Council approved a hiring preference within the Department of Human Resources for children and spouses of Chicago first responders who were killed in the line of duty.

Council adopted a resolution designating July as Disability Pride Month in Chicago.

Council also adopted a resolution calling for Andrew Boutros to resign as U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of Illinois and calling for a Department of Justice review of obligations connected to Operation Midway Blitz, Broadview prosecutions, and related proceedings.

Several honorary street designations were approved, including Mamie and E.L. Till Road, Honorary Jermaine A. Pelt Way, Honorary Terry Kath Way, and others.

Council also approved changes to liquor-license restrictions in several parts of the city.

The bigger picture

A City Council meeting can contain hundreds of individual pieces of legislation. Many are hyperlocal — a parking restriction on one block, a sign permit for one business, or an honorary street designation.

But taken together, the July 15 meeting also shows some of the larger policy questions Chicago is dealing with.

How should Chicago approach public safety technology?

How should a city built around street parking transition toward electric vehicles?

What should Chicago do with publicly owned vacant land?

How can neighborhood development create housing and public spaces without leaving existing residents behind?

And perhaps most importantly: how much say should Chicagoans have in those decisions?

The individual ordinances may look technical. Their effects are not.
